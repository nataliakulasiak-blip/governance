The Esquilino

twenty-five centuries, point by point

The district

The hill of those on the outside

We have worked for sixty years on a street with Rome's burial ground under its feet, along with the garden of Maecenas and the baths of Trajan. We had the story told to us, and we put it in order: 17 points, from the earthworks of the city wall to this morning's market.

  1. The name

    Those on the outside

    Esquilino perhaps comes from exquiliae: those who live outside, beyond the built-up city. The hill is born with the name of the people who live at its edges, and for twenty-five centuries it never quite stops resembling them.

  2. 4th century BC

    The wall and the agger

    Here the hill is flat and undefended: a wall is not enough. The Romans raise an enormous earthwork, the agger, and set against it tufa blocks the size of a man.

    Its remains can still be seen in the gardens of piazza Vittorio and outside Termini station.

  3. 2nd–1st century BC

    The field of the dead

    Outside the wall the Esquilino is Rome's burial ground: the common pits of the poor and of slaves, the puticuli. Horace tells of it in the eighth satire of his first book.

    “Here stood the common grave of the wretched poor.”

  4. Around 35 BC

    The Gardens of Maecenas

    Maecenas, the friend of Augustus, buys the field of the dead and plants a garden over it. Rome's most infamous quarter becomes its most sought-after. Horace and Virgil walk in it.

    The Auditorium of Maecenas, in largo Leopardi, came back to light in 1874: a sunken hall with a garden painted on its walls.

  5. AD 64

    The fire and the Domus Aurea

    The great fire burns Rome for nine days. Nero takes the Oppian hill, a spur of the Esquilino, and builds the Domus Aurea on it.

  6. AD 109

    The Baths of Trajan

    Trajan buries Nero's house and founds his baths on top of it. That is why the Domus Aurea has come down to us: it was buried, not demolished.

  7. AD 262

    The Arch of Gallienus

    The old Porta Esquilina of the city wall is faced in travertine and dedicated to the emperor Gallienus. It is still there, wedged between two houses, on via di San Vito.

  8. 432–440

    Santa Maria Maggiore

    Pope Sixtus III raises the basilica right after the Council of Ephesus. The mosaics of the nave date from then: sixteen hundred years, never redone.

    The legend of the August snow — the Madonna tracing the outline of the church in snow — will arrive many centuries later.

  9. From the 6th century

    The vineyards

    With the aqueducts cut during the Gothic war, the hills are left without water and empty out. Rome comes down to the Tiber and the Esquilino goes back to being countryside: vineyards and vegetable gardens for a thousand years, with the basilica in the middle like an island.

  10. Around 1288

    The Cosmatesque floor

    In Santa Maria Maggiore the Roman marble-workers inlay the floor: discs of porphyry and serpentine, ribbons of tesserae, rosettes. It is the pattern this site carries beneath its cover image and at the foot of every page.

    They took the marble from the ancient ruins: a hill that recycles itself.

  11. 1587

    Sixtus V brings the water back

    In two years Pope Sixtus V brings in the Acqua Felice, plants the obelisk behind the apse of the basilica, and has Domenico Fontana drive through the straight streets that still cut across the district today.

    His villa, the Montalto, would cover the Esquilino until the nineteenth century: it was levelled to make room for Termini station.

  12. Between 1655 and 1680

    The Magic Door

    The Marquis Palombara had a page of alchemical symbols carved on the door of his villa. The villa is gone; the door is not, in the gardens of piazza Vittorio. Nobody has ever fully deciphered it.

  13. 1871–1888

    Piazza Vittorio

    Rome becomes the capital and the Esquilino fills with apartment blocks in twenty years. Piazza Vittorio Emanuele II is laid out, with the longest arcades in the city, and with it our own street: via Principe Amedeo.

    So many clerks arrive from Piedmont that the district takes its nickname from them.

  14. 1874

    The nymphaeum found again

    Digging in piazza Vittorio brings up a brick ruin that Romans have always called the Trophies of Marius. It has nothing to do with Marius: it is a third-century nymphaeum. The marble trophies had already been carried off to the Capitol by Sixtus V.

  15. 1962

    Alfredo

    At number 126 of via Principe Amedeo a family trattoria opens. A wooden door, sand-coloured awnings, no illuminated sign. Sixty years later it is still there.

  16. 2001

    The Nuovo Mercato Esquilino

    The market leaves the square and moves under the roof of a former barracks, between via Filippo Turati and our own street. Since then we have done our shopping two hundred metres from home.

  17. Today

    The district that cooks the world

    On the stalls, pecorino and cardamom sit ten metres apart. We remain an orthodox Roman kitchen, but it is impossible to work here and learn nothing from the stall next door.

The district tells itself best at the table.
